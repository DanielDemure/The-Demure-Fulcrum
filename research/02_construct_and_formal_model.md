# Construct Definition, Formal Model, and Empirical Designs

**Research companion to *The Demure Fulcrum***  
**Status:** Proposed operationalisation — not a validated measurement instrument

## 1. Research problem

The Demure Fulcrum is scientifically useful only if researchers can distinguish it from ordinary bargaining, assertiveness, persuasion, problem-focused coping, affiliation, and appeasement. This document expands the strict definition, specifies inclusion and exclusion rules, formalises the `G → G′` distinction, and proposes measurement and experimental designs.

The unit of analysis is a **response episode**: a bounded interval beginning when an actor recognises an adverse prospect and ending when the actor exits, accepts, is forced into an outcome, reaches an agreement, or the situation is otherwise transformed.

## 2. Strict inclusion rules

Code an episode as strict negotiation under threat only when all conditions below are evidenced in behaviour or the documented interaction.

### 2.1 Adverse prospect

The actor faces a meaningful risk, conflict, loss, constraint, or threatened deterioration. Everyday low-stakes bargaining can be studied as a comparison condition, but the focal construct concerns responses to perceived threat or consequential adversity.

### 2.2 Influenceable counterpart or process

A person, group, organisation, or institutional procedure must be capable of changing at least part of the outcome. The counterpart can be distributed—for example, an employer represented by a manager and human-resources process—but must be more than an imagined force.

### 2.3 Partial interdependence

The parties control different resources, approvals, information, actions, or commitments. Complete independence leaves nothing to negotiate. Complete unilateral control reduces the interaction toward command or compliance, although institutional appeals may reopen interdependence.

### 2.4 Signalling

At least one signal concerning interests, constraints, intentions, credibility, priorities, alternatives, or proposed terms must be transmitted. Silence may be strategic within a larger exchange but is not sufficient by itself.

### 2.5 Contingent structure

The response must link one party's action to another's response: “If you do X, I will do Y,” “I can accept A if B changes,” “Before deciding, I need information C,” or an equivalent offer, request, commitment, package, or procedural proposal.

### 2.6 Attempted transformation

The target is a change in options, information, commitments, timing, participants, enforcement, interpretation, or payoff distribution. A request to choose one existing option without changing any term may be ordinary decision making rather than a meta-response.

### 2.7 Residual agency

The actor retains some meaningful ability to represent interests and respond conditionally. This can include counterproposal, delay, appeal, coalition, refusal, exit, or a fallback. Residual agency is graded and may be small.

## 3. Exclusion and boundary rules

Do not code an episode as strict negotiation when:

- the “counterpart” cannot perceive or affect the outcome;
- the interaction contains expression but no contingency;
- the actor only changes private interpretation;
- a request is purely informational and no terms are at stake;
- compliance is unconditional and no alternative is preserved;
- a threat is so immediate that no exchange occurs;
- the episode is reconstructed only from an inspirational story without behavioural evidence.

### 3.1 Persuasion boundary

Persuasion attempts to change belief or action through reasons, framing, or rhetoric. It becomes negotiation when the interaction includes reciprocal contingency or an attempted change in terms. A lawyer's argument to a judge may be persuasion within an institutional process; a plea agreement is negotiation.

### 3.2 Mediation boundary

A mediator does not necessarily negotiate for personal interests but can enable negotiation by changing information, sequence, process, or commitments. Code the parties' response episodes separately from the mediator's intervention.

### 3.3 Coercion boundary

A coercive offer can contain genuine contingency while offering almost no acceptable alternative. Code bargaining form and residual agency separately. This avoids calling an imposed ultimatum either full negotiation or no interaction at all.

### 3.4 Four-form typology and coding consequence

The framework distinguishes four forms because they require different units of analysis.

| Form | Unit of analysis | Core coding consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Interpersonal | exchange among responsive external agents | code offers, counteroffers, information, concessions, commitments, and alternatives |
| Institutional | exchange through authorised rules, representatives, appeals, or multi-actor procedures | code who has authority, how the procedure can change terms, and where commitments are stored or enforced |
| Intrapersonal | interaction among goals, values, identities, or temporal selves | do not score as strict negotiation; study as a self-regulation extension |
| Existential | narrative engagement with fate, death, God, meaning, or an imagined absolute | do not score as strict negotiation; study phenomenology and cultural meaning |

Interpersonal and institutional episodes may be combined for some analyses only after testing whether their measurement structure is equivalent. Institutional cases may distribute authority and memory across several nodes, making “the counterpart” a process rather than one person. Intrapersonal and existential cases should never be used as positive examples in a confirmatory scale for the strict construct.

## 4. Formal representation

Represent the initial interaction as:

```
G = <A, S, O, U, I, C, τ>
```

- `A`: actors and decision authorities;
- `S`: relevant states of the world;
- `O`: currently available options;
- `U`: values, interests, and payoff functions;
- `I`: information, beliefs, and uncertainty;
- `C`: commitments, rules, enforcement, and institutional constraints;
- `τ`: timing, sequencing, and deadlines.

A first-order policy chooses `o ∈ O(G)`. A meta-response sends a signal or proposal `m` intended to induce:

```
T_m(G) = G′
```

A transformation may affect multiple components. Revealing a hidden deadline changes `I`; offering a guarantee changes `C`; adding a trial period changes `τ` and `O`; involving a mediator changes `A`, `I`, and potentially `C`; packaging issues can change perceived `U` and expand the feasible set [@nash1950; @rubinstein1982; @raiffa1982; @sebenius1992].

## 5. Negotiability dimensions

A provisional situation-level index should be developed separately from person-level response tendencies.

| Dimension | Low negotiability | High negotiability | Possible manipulation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Counterpart influenceability | no actor can change outcome | identified actor controls relevant term | automated fixed rule versus authorised representative |
| Communication | no channel or one-way command | reciprocal channel | messaging disabled versus enabled |
| Interdependence | no exchange value | each side controls something valued | remove versus introduce complementary resources |
| Time | immediate irreversible outcome | pause or multiple rounds | 10 seconds versus 5 minutes |
| Commitment | promises not credible | guarantee, contract, reputation, or enforcement | unenforceable versus verified commitment |
| Information | no relevant private information | priorities or constraints can be exchanged | common information versus asymmetric information |
| Alternatives | no fallback or appeal | exit, appeal, coalition, or alternative partner | single gatekeeper versus second channel |

The index should not be assumed additive. Communication may have little effect if the counterpart lacks authority; time may matter only when a proposal can change something.

## 6. Behavioural codebook

### 6.1 Core codes

1. **Interest statement:** identifies a need, value, concern, or priority.
2. **Constraint disclosure:** reveals a limit, deadline, authority boundary, or resource constraint.
3. **Information request:** seeks priorities, reasons, authority, alternatives, or hidden constraints.
4. **Offer:** proposes terms linked to acceptance.
5. **Counteroffer:** modifies or rejects terms while preserving exchange.
6. **Conditional concession:** gives something only in relation to a reciprocal move.
7. **Unconditional concession:** yields without requested reciprocity.
8. **Option creation:** introduces a package, trial, sequence, division, exception, or new resource.
9. **Procedural move:** requests pause, mediation, appeal, written terms, or decision review.
10. **Alternative preservation:** names or protects an exit, fallback, coalition, or later option.
11. **Threat or coercive signal:** raises the cost of non-compliance.
12. **Exit:** terminates the interaction.

### 6.2 Episode-level ratings

- degree of adverse stakes;
- degree of power asymmetry;
- degree of residual agency;
- attempted structural transformation;
- reciprocity of exchange;
- outcome: agreement, improved terms, unchanged terms, deterioration, exit, force, or unresolved;
- safety consequences;
- subjective agency before and after the episode.

### 6.3 Reliability target

Two or more blinded coders should independently code episodes. Categorical codes should reach acceptable agreement before confirmatory use. Low agreement should trigger revision of definitions rather than statistical averaging of ambiguous judgments.

## 7. Candidate scale development

A self-report instrument must not consist solely of aspirational statements such as “I always find a way.” Items should refer to specific tendencies under adversity and should separate capability, opportunity, and enacted behaviour.

Candidate item domains:

- I identify who can actually change the relevant terms.
- I ask what the other party is constrained by before proposing a solution.
- I make concessions conditional rather than automatic.
- I look for an appeal, alternative, or pause before accepting an imposed outcome.
- Under threat, I can state my interests without assuming the other side will agree.
- I generate new packages or sequences rather than choosing only among presented options.
- I distinguish situations that can be negotiated from those requiring immediate safety action.

Reverse-scored items should target conceptual opposites, not social desirability:

- Once options are presented by an authority, I rarely consider whether the options themselves can change.
- I accept concessions immediately when conflict feels dangerous, even when a counterproposal is possible.

Scale work should include cognitive interviews, item-response analysis, exploratory factor analysis, confirmatory replication, measurement invariance, test-retest reliability, and behavioural prediction. It should compare with coping, perceived control, assertiveness, reappraisal, trait anxiety, social problem solving, and negotiation confidence [@carver1989; @skinner1996; @gross1998; @curhan2006].

## 8. Experimental programme

### 8.1 Experiment A: Negotiability factorial

**Design:** 2 × 2 between-participant design.

- counterpart influenceability: fixed algorithm versus authorised human representative;
- reciprocal communication: unavailable versus available.

Participants face a consequential allocation or deadline problem. Objective stakes and presented options remain constant.

**Primary outcomes:** proportion making a contingent proposal; number of information requests; option creation; acceptance, exit, or compliance.

**Prediction:** strict negotiation should be most frequent when both an influenceable counterpart and reciprocal communication are present. Communication alone should not create a large effect when no authority exists.

**Alternative explanation:** communication may merely increase expression. The primary outcome must therefore require contingency and attempted structural change.

### 8.2 Experiment B: Time and threat imminence

**Design:** manipulate decision time while holding stakes constant.

- immediate decision with 15 seconds;
- protected pause with three minutes;
- protected pause plus a negotiation checklist.

**Prediction:** strict negotiation should increase with time and support, especially for participants who otherwise default to acceptance or exit. Generic planning performance should be measured as a comparison.

### 8.3 Experiment C: Appeasement versus negotiation under power asymmetry

Participants interact with a high-power counterpart who can impose a loss but also needs a resource held by the participant. Manipulate whether that dependency is visible.

**Prediction:** visible interdependence should increase counterproposals and conditional concessions. When dependency is hidden, unilateral concession should increase.

Safety and ethics require simulated stakes, transparent debriefing, and exclusion of participants for whom coercive scenarios present undue risk.

### 8.4 Experiment D: Transformability versus direct control

Cross direct control with negotiability:

- high direct control, low negotiability;
- low direct control, high negotiability;
- both high;
- both low.

This separates “I can solve it myself” from “I can alter terms through another actor.” The Demure Fulcrum predicts negotiation selection from the second dimension after control is accounted for.

### 8.5 Experiment E: Incremental validity

Collect baseline measures of problem-focused coping, perceived control, assertiveness, reappraisal, negotiation experience, trait anxiety, and cognitive reflection. Use preregistered multilevel models to predict behaviour across scenarios.

Evidence for distinctiveness requires out-of-sample improvement in prediction from Demure Fulcrum indicators. A statistically significant coefficient without meaningful predictive gain is insufficient.

### 8.6 Secondary exploratory studies

These studies are downstream of Experiments A–E and should not be launched as searches for a unique biomarker before construct validity exists.

#### Study F: expert cue detection and automaticity

Compare experienced negotiators with matched controls on validated scenarios containing or withholding influenceability, contingency, and transformability. Measure behavioural latency and accuracy first; EEG may then examine timing. The critical test is selective detection of negotiability, not a generic expert speed advantage.

#### Study G: developmental differentiation

Use age-appropriate interactive conflicts to separate requests, persuasion, sharing, imitation, appeasement, and contingent exchange. Model language, executive function, perspective taking, and metacognition [@flavell1979]. Do not preregister a fixed age boundary as though the construct were one maturational switch.

#### Study H: cross-cultural portability

Translate the codebook through collaborative adaptation, not literal wording alone. Compare direct and indirect signalling, individual and collective representation, and mediator-led processes. Test coder reliability and measurement invariance; treat failure as evidence for localisation or construct revision.

#### Study I: psychophysiological constraints

After behavioural validity, compare negotiation with effort-matched social problem solving, persuasion, and non-social control conditions. Measure arousal, EEG, endocrine response, or imaging only to constrain mechanisms. No region or hormone is assumed diagnostic.

#### Study J: dynamic-network strategy comparison

Formalise a negotiation-inspired strategy independently of the Hexure name. Compare it with Always Defect, Always Cooperate, Tit-for-Tat, Generous Tit-for-Tat, Grim Trigger, Win-Stay-Lose-Shift, and partner-choice baselines across topology, noise, rewiring cost, information quality, and payoff regimes. Preregister seeds, primary outcomes, sensitivity analyses, adverse outcomes, and out-of-sample replications. A tuned win under one parameter set is not evidence of general superiority.

The current pre-registration-ready specification is [`../simulations/hexure_ipd_protocol.md`](../simulations/hexure_ipd_protocol.md). It treats run-level summaries as the inferential unit, includes mechanism ablations and held-out environments, and requires rejection when gains depend on unrealistic information, cost-free rewiring, exclusion, false sanction, or concentration.

## 9. Analysis plan principles

- preregister primary outcomes and exclusion rules;
- separate exploratory scale construction from confirmatory testing;
- report effect sizes and uncertainty, not only significance;
- model repeated scenarios within participants;
- test interactions rather than assuming a universal main effect of stress;
- include manipulation checks for perceived authority, communication, control, and threat;
- publish null and adverse findings;
- test whether coding works across languages and institutional contexts.

## 10. Rival models

### Active-coping model

Negotiation is simply one instance of problem-focused coping. The framework survives only if its structural variables explain when this particular response appears.

### Assertiveness model

Agency preservation may reduce to direct self-expression. Counterevidence would require cases in which quiet procedural or package-based negotiation is coded correctly and direct assertion without contingency is excluded.

### Social-approach model

Negotiation may be a form of affiliation or approach motivation. Hostile distributive bargaining and mediated institutional appeals provide tests because they involve structural exchange without warmth.

### Skill-and-opportunity model

No new response construct is needed: people negotiate when opportunity exists and they possess skill. This may ultimately be the best model. The Demure Fulcrum must show whether “meta-response selection under threat” adds explanatory organisation beyond that interaction.

### Power-resource model

What appears to be agency is simply leverage produced by money, status, information, or alternatives. Research should measure objective resources and examine whether the proposed cognitive and behavioural sequence adds anything.

## 11. Criteria for revision or rejection

Revise the construct if the strict criteria exclude important recurring cases, if agency proves multidimensional, or if cultural contexts rely on implicit rather than explicit contingency. Reject the distinctiveness claim if coding is unreliable, experimental negotiability does not affect behaviour, or established variables fully account for outcomes.

The strongest possible result is not universal confirmation. It is a boundary-rich model that predicts where negotiation appears, where it does not, and when another response is safer or more effective.
