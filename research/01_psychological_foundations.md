# Psychological and Neuroscientific Foundations for a Fourth Threat Response: A Literature Review

**Report Commissioned for Foundational Research on the Demure Fulcrum Theory**
**Date of Publication: 2026-01-02**

## Introduction

The human response to perceived threats has long been a cornerstone of psychological and neuroscientific inquiry. The canonical "fight-or-flight" model, while foundational, has undergone significant revision and expansion over the past century to better capture the complexity of survival-oriented behaviors. This academic literature review seeks to provide a comprehensive examination of the psychological and neuroscientific foundations that may support the proposition of a fourth, uniquely human threat response category, conceptualized as 'Negotiate' within the Demure Fulcrum theory. To achieve this, the report will first document the historical evolution of the fight-flight-freeze model, tracing its origins from Walter Cannon's pioneering work. It will then analyze documented fourth-response proposals, such as 'fawn' and 'tend-and-befriend,' comparing their characteristics to the cognitive and social requirements of a negotiation-based strategy. Further, this review will explore the explanatory power of Stephen Porges' Polyvagal Theory, with a particular focus on its Social Engagement System, as a potential neurobiological substrate for such a response. The critical role of the prefrontal cortex in emotional regulation, cognitive reappraisal, and metacognition will be examined as the neurological architecture enabling the conscious, goal-directed action inherent in negotiation. Finally, the report will address established academic critiques of the traditional tripartite model, contextualizing the need for more nuanced frameworks that can accommodate the full spectrum of human adaptation to stress and threat. This synthesis aims to build a robust, evidence-based foundation for future academic discourse on advanced, socially mediated survival strategies.

## The Historical Evolution of Threat Response Models

The conceptualization of organismic responses to acute stress began in the early 20th century with the work of American physiologist Walter Bradford Cannon. Cannon first described the "fight-or-flight" response, a term he coined, in the 1910s, culminating in his influential 1915 book, *Bodily Changes in Pain, Hunger, Fear and Rage* [4, 6]. His theory posited that in the face of a perceived threat, animals undergo a general and rapid discharge of the sympathetic nervous system, preparing the body for the energetic demands of either direct confrontation or rapid escape [1]. Cannon's research elucidated the function of what he termed the "sympathico-adrenal system," demonstrating how the sympathetic nervous system and the adrenal glands work in concert to orchestrate a cascade of physiological changes [4]. This includes the release of hormones like adrenaline (epinephrine) and cortisol, which mobilize energy reserves by increasing blood sugar, elevate heart rate and blood pressure, and redirect blood flow to the muscles and brain, all in service of a "violent display of energy" [1, 4]. Cannon's work laid the essential groundwork for stress physiology, establishing a direct link between psychological perception of threat and a profound, systemic bodily reaction [2].

Over time, the elegant simplicity of the fight-or-flight dichotomy was recognized as incomplete. Researchers and ethologists observed that many species, when confronted with a threat, exhibit an initial period of immobility. This led to the formal inclusion of "freeze" as a third primary acute stress response, creating the now widely recognized "fight-flight-freeze" (3F) model [1, 5]. The freeze response, characterized by a state of hypervigilant stillness, is not merely passive inaction. It is an active, adaptive survival strategy intended to reduce the likelihood of detection by a predator [5]. Neurobiologically, this state is distinct from the sympathetic arousal of fight or flight; it often involves co-activation of the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems, leading to a state of simultaneous readiness and inhibition, sometimes described as putting one foot on the gas and the other on the brake [3, 5]. This expansion acknowledged a more complex "defense cascade," where the specific response is often dependent on the proximity and nature of the threat. Freezing is typically an initial response to a distal threat, allowing for assessment, while fight or flight are deployed as the threat becomes more proximal and inescapable [3, 24]. This evolution from a two-part to a three-part model marked a critical step toward a more nuanced understanding of the dynamic and context-dependent nature of survival instincts.

## Critiques and Expansions of the Tripartite Model

Despite its widespread adoption, the fight-flight-freeze model has faced academic critique for being an oversimplification that fails to capture the full spectrum of defensive behaviors observed across species, including humans [3]. Contemporary research, particularly within frameworks like the revised Reinforcement Sensitivity Theory (r-RST), has sought to build more comprehensive models [26]. A key contribution of r-RST is the functional and neurological separation of fear and anxiety. In this view, the Fight-Flight-Freeze System (FFFS) is specifically associated with the emotion of fear and is activated by clear and present threats, mediating defensive avoidance [23, 26]. In contrast, the Behavioral Inhibition System (BIS) is associated with anxiety and is activated by goal conflict and potential threats, leading to risk assessment, behavioral inhibition, and increased arousal [26]. This distinction highlights that not all aversive responses are the same; they are mediated by different neural systems and triggered by different contextual cues, such as the perceived "defensive distance" from a threat [26].

This has led to the development of the "defense cascade" model, which provides a more granular continuum of innate, automatically activated defense reactions that extend beyond the basic three [24]. This cascade begins with arousal and often proceeds to freezing, which is conceptualized as an inhibited fight-or-flight state characterized by parasympathetically dominated heart rate deceleration [24]. If the threat persists and escape or confrontation is possible, the organism shifts to active defense (flight or fight), marked by sympathetically driven heart rate acceleration [24]. However, if the threat is perceived as inescapable and active defenses fail, the cascade can progress to more extreme states of passive immobility. These include tonic immobility, a state of motor inhibition often seen in prey animals, and collapsed immobility, a more profound shutdown characterized by a dramatic drop in heart rate and muscle tone, which can lead to fainting [24]. Each stage of this cascade is associated with a distinct neural pattern involving a common pathway that includes the amygdala, hypothalamus, and periaqueductal gray (PAG) [24]. This expanded model underscores that threat response is not a simple choice between three options but a hierarchical and sequential process, influenced by individual differences, cognitive appraisals, and the escalating nature of the threatening encounter.

## The Emergence of Socially-Oriented Fourth Responses

Further challenging the sufficiency of the 3F model, researchers have identified additional stress responses that are fundamentally social in nature. Two of the most prominent are the "tend-and-befriend" response and the "fawn" response. The tend-and-befriend model, proposed by Shelley Taylor and her colleagues in 2000, describes a biobehavioral strategy for coping with stress that is centered on social connection [8]. It involves two primary components: "tending," which refers to nurturing activities designed to protect offspring from harm, and "befriending," which involves seeking and building social networks to provide mutual defense and support [7, 8]. Taylor's research suggests this response is more common in females, proposing a biological basis linked to the interplay of hormones. The release of oxytocin, a hormone associated with bonding and trust, in combination with female reproductive hormones like estrogen, may buffer the classic fight-or-flight response and instead promote affiliative behaviors [7, 8]. From an evolutionary perspective, this strategy would have conferred a survival advantage by fostering a protective social fabric, enhancing the safety of both individuals and their young. Importantly, tend-and-befriend is framed as a healthy, adaptive survival strategy rooted in the benefits of social connection for security and resilience [7].

In contrast, the "fawn" response, a term introduced by psychotherapist Pete Walker, describes a survival strategy that also involves social appeasement but originates from a context of trauma, particularly chronic childhood abuse or neglect [12, 14]. Fawning is characterized by an attempt to ensure safety by placating a threat, anticipating the needs and demands of an aggressor, and suppressing one's own feelings, needs, and boundaries to avoid conflict [12, 13, 14]. This pattern of people-pleasing and codependency is learned as a survival mechanism in an environment where asserting oneself could lead to retaliation or further harm [9, 13]. While it is an adaptive strategy within a dangerous relational context, it often becomes a maladaptive and deeply ingrained pattern in adulthood, leading to a diminished sense of self, difficulty setting boundaries, and vulnerability in relationships [10, 12]. Though both tend-and-befriend and fawn involve social engagement under duress, they are critically different. Tend-and-befriend is a proactive, biologically-driven strategy for building collective security, whereas fawning is a reactive, trauma-based coping mechanism focused on the appeasement of a specific threat at the cost of self-abandonment [7]. This distinction highlights how socially-oriented responses can be either adaptive and health-promoting or maladaptive and rooted in pathology.

## Neurobiological Substrates for Social Engagement and Regulation

The exploration of socially-oriented threat responses necessitates an examination of the underlying neurobiological architecture that makes them possible. Stephen Porges' Polyvagal Theory offers a compelling, albeit debated, framework for understanding the connection between the autonomic nervous system (ANS) and social behavior [15, 18]. The theory posits a hierarchical, three-part organization of the ANS that evolved to support different survival strategies [17, 18]. The most primitive circuit is the unmyelinated dorsal vagal complex, which governs the immobilization or "shutdown" response when an organism is overwhelmed. The next level is the sympathetic nervous system, responsible for the mobilization required for fight-or-flight behaviors. The most recently evolved and, according to the theory, uniquely mammalian circuit is the myelinated ventral vagal complex (VVC) [17]. This VVC forms the core of the **Social Engagement System** [15, 17]. It regulates the muscles of the face and head, enabling the facial expressions, vocal intonations, and listening capabilities that are crucial for social communication [17]. When the VVC is active, it promotes a state of calm and connection, downregulating defensive sympathetic or dorsal vagal responses. This state supports health, growth, and restoration, and makes individuals feel safe enough to engage with others. A key concept in the theory is **neuroception**, the nervous system's subconscious process of scanning for cues of safety or danger in the environment and from other people [19, 20]. Safe social engagement, facilitated by the VVC, depends on the neuroceptive detection of safety cues, which allows for **co-regulation**, the mutual, reciprocal process of calming and connecting nervous systems [15, 16]. While Polyvagal Theory has been influential in clinical settings, it has faced academic criticism regarding some of its specific evolutionary and neuroanatomical claims [18, 22]. Critics have challenged the proposed uniqueness of the mammalian myelinated vagus and the strict hierarchical relationship between the three systems. Nevertheless, the theory's central insight—that the ANS is deeply intertwined with social behavior and that a specific neural circuit exists to facilitate feelings of safety and connection—provides a powerful biological rationale for prosocial responses to stress.

Beyond the autonomic nervous system, the capacity for advanced, flexible threat responses is critically dependent on the prefrontal cortex (PFC), the most highly evolved region of the brain. The PFC exerts top-down regulatory control over more primitive, subcortical structures like the amygdala, which is central to threat detection and the initiation of fear responses [28]. One of the key mechanisms for this regulation is **cognitive reappraisal**, an emotion regulation strategy that involves reinterpreting the meaning of a stimulus to change its emotional impact [40, 41]. Neuroimaging studies consistently show that engaging in cognitive reappraisal activates regions of the PFC, such as the dorsolateral and ventrolateral PFC, which are associated with cognitive control. This prefrontal activation, in turn, modulates and typically reduces activity in the amygdala, effectively dampening the emotional response [40, 41, 43]. The precise pathway for this modulation is still under investigation, with some models suggesting the PFC influences the amygdala indirectly by engaging the ventromedial PFC (a region involved in signaling safety), while others propose it works by altering semantic representations of the stimulus in the temporal cortex [40, 41]. This capacity for cognitive reappraisal is a cornerstone of human adaptability, allowing individuals to consciously shift their perspective and manage emotional reactions rather than being governed by automatic impulses.

This prefrontal capacity underpins even more complex human abilities like **metacognition**, or "thinking about thinking" [38]. Metacognition allows for the monitoring and control of one's own cognitive processes, a level of self-awareness that is profoundly developed in humans due to the significant expansion of our anterior prefrontal cortex [30, 31]. This ability is fundamental to planning, reasoning, and understanding the mental states of others (Theory of Mind) [39]. In the context of a threat, metacognition enables an individual to move beyond reflexive reaction. It allows for the conscious evaluation of the situation, the consideration of long-term consequences, the formulation of complex strategies, and the regulation of one's own internal state to achieve a desired outcome. While animals possess regulatory PFC mechanisms, the human capacity for explicit, conscious metacognition and abstract reasoning provides the foundation for strategies that are not merely reactive but proactive, goal-directed, and socially sophisticated.

## Synthesizing a Foundation for a 'Negotiate' Response

The preceding analysis of threat response models and their neurobiological underpinnings provides a fertile ground for conceptualizing 'Negotiate' as a distinct, fourth category of human stress response. Unlike the instinctual, largely automatic nature of fight, flight, and freeze, and distinct from the specific social patterns of tend-and-befriend or fawn, negotiation represents a highly evolved, cognitively mediated strategy. Research into negotiation as a coping mechanism frames it as a form of problem-focused coping, an active approach that involves defining a problem, generating solutions, and taking action to manage external demands that are appraised as stressful [33, 34]. The effectiveness of negotiation is deeply tied to cognitive appraisal; perceiving a conflict as a "challenge" rather than a "threat" leads to better outcomes and lower stress [35]. This aligns directly with the function of cognitive reappraisal, where prefrontal systems reframe a stimulus to mitigate its negative emotional impact and enable more constructive action.

A 'Negotiate' response can be differentiated from other proposed fourth responses. While 'tend-and-befriend' is a prosocial, adaptive strategy, it is described as a biobehavioral drive for creating safety through affiliation and mutual defense, often linked to specific hormonal profiles. 'Fawn' is a trauma response of appeasement, sacrificing self-interest for immediate safety. 'Negotiate', in contrast, implies a metacognitive process of strategic engagement aimed at achieving a mutually acceptable outcome, or a win-win. It requires the capacities subserved by the most advanced neural circuits: the Social Engagement System described by Polyvagal Theory to establish a baseline of safety and trust necessary for dialogue, and the prefrontal cortex to execute complex cognitive functions. These functions include emotional regulation to prevent amygdala-driven hijacking, working memory to hold multiple perspectives in mind, cognitive flexibility to generate novel solutions, and Theory of Mind to understand the intentions and needs of the other party. Therefore, 'Negotiate' is not simply a behavior but a meta-strategy that leverages our most sophisticated neural hardware.

From an evolutionary and developmental perspective, this response represents the pinnacle of the defense cascade. Where fight, flight, and freeze are primitive survival mechanisms, and even tend-and-befriend is a more fundamental drive for group safety, negotiation is a uniquely human adaptation for navigating the complex social world we have created. It is a conscious choice to override instinctual impulses in favor of a higher-order, goal-directed process. This requires the ability to self-regulate, delay gratification, and engage in abstract thought about future possibilities. The psychological factors that enhance negotiation effectiveness—such as mindfulness, emotional intelligence, and epistemic motivation (the drive to seek information)—are all functions of a well-regulated and highly integrated nervous system, where the reflective capacities of the prefrontal cortex are fully engaged and in control of more reactive, subcortical systems [32]. Thus, the 'Negotiate' response is not merely another option in the threat repertoire; it is the expression of human agency, a conscious and deliberate act of creating a solution rather than simply reacting to a problem.

## Conclusion

The traditional fight-flight-freeze model, while a vital starting point, is insufficient to encapsulate the full range of human responses to threat and stress. Academic inquiry has progressively expanded this framework, first by incorporating a more nuanced understanding of immobility states within a "defense cascade," and second by identifying socially-oriented strategies like the adaptive 'tend-and-befriend' and the trauma-based 'fawn' response. This review has synthesized evidence from neuroscience and psychology to argue that a robust foundation exists for considering 'Negotiate' as a distinct, highly evolved fourth response category. The neurobiological architecture for such a strategy is evident in the interplay between two key systems. Stephen Porges' Polyvagal Theory, despite its critics, provides a compelling model for a Social Engagement System, mediated by the ventral vagal complex, which creates the physiological state of safety and connection necessary for constructive dialogue. Concurrently, the human prefrontal cortex provides the top-down regulatory capacity for cognitive reappraisal and metacognition, allowing individuals to override primitive impulses, manage emotional reactivity, and engage in the complex, goal-directed planning that negotiation requires. Unlike other responses, 'Negotiate' is not primarily an instinctual reaction but a conscious, cognitively demanding meta-strategy. It represents a shift from reactive survival to proactive problem-solving, leveraging our most sophisticated neural capacities to navigate conflict and create mutually beneficial outcomes. Further research into the Demure Fulcrum theory may therefore illuminate how this uniquely human capacity for negotiation functions as a fundamental mechanism for not only survival, but also for fostering agency, meaning, and social progress.
