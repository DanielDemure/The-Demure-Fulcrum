# Threat Response, Appraisal, and Regulation

**Research companion to *The Demure Fulcrum***  
**Status:** Scholarly background review — not an independent validation of the framework

## Purpose

This companion summarises research that constrains any account of negotiation under threat. It asks what is known about defensive behaviour, appraisal, control, stress-related cognitive change, social responses, and emotion regulation. The literature can show which mechanisms are plausible and which claims would be excessive. It cannot by itself establish the Demure Fulcrum as a distinct construct.

## 1. From fight-or-flight to dynamic defensive systems

Walter Cannon's account of bodily mobilisation under threat remains historically important [@cannon1915]. Modern research, however, does not support a simple menu of three mutually exclusive responses. Expanded stress-response lists and defence-cascade models are themselves heuristics rather than evidence for neatly separated modules [@bracha2004; @kozlowska2015]. Defensive behaviour varies with threat distance, escape availability, prior learning, environmental affordances, and the perceived cost of action [@fanselow1994; @blanchard2001; @mobbs2007].

The defense-cascade literature describes linked phases that can include orientation, freezing, active escape or confrontation, tonic immobility, and collapsed immobility [@kozlowska2015]. Freezing is not always passive failure. It can reduce detection, stabilise perception, and prepare action [@roelofs2017]. The same outward stillness may therefore have different functions depending on timing and context.

This has two implications for the Demure Fulcrum. First, negotiation should not be inserted into an oversimplified list as though each label named an equivalent biological module. Second, an apparent pause before negotiation may reflect orientation or action preparation, but the presence of a pause alone is not evidence of negotiation. The defining evidence must be the later contingent attempt to transform the situation.

## 2. Threat, fear, anxiety, and conscious experience

Contemporary affective neuroscience distinguishes defensive survival circuits from the conscious experience and report of fear or anxiety [@ledoux2012; @ledouxpine2016]. Rapid threat processing can shape behaviour before a person articulates an interpretation, while conscious appraisal, memory, expectation, and language also influence what is perceived as threatening.

The distinction matters because negotiation is usually described at the level of purposeful behaviour. A person may experience fear while negotiating, may report calm while using coercion, or may act from a learned script with little conscious deliberation. The framework should therefore avoid defining negotiation by one feeling state.

## 3. Stress physiology as constraint, not destiny

Stress mediators can be adaptive in the short term and damaging when activation is excessive or prolonged [@mcewen1998]. Acute stress changes network dynamics and can shift the balance between salience detection, executive control, memory, and habitual action [@hermans2014]. Prefrontal functions are particularly sensitive to uncontrollable or intense stress [@arnsten2009].

Meta-analytic evidence suggests that acute stress can impair working memory, inhibition, and cognitive flexibility, although effects vary by task, timing, cortisol response, and participant characteristics [@shields2016]. Stress can also alter risk taking and decision strategy [@porcelli2009; @starcke2012]. These findings argue against a motivational slogan in which anyone can simply choose a reflective response regardless of conditions.

For the Demure Fulcrum, stress should be modelled as a moderator. Negotiation is likely to become harder when stress removes time, narrows attention, consumes working memory, makes the counterpart appear wholly hostile, or hides alternatives. Training, preparation, social support, external decision aids, and institutional pauses may preserve the capacity to negotiate without eliminating arousal.

## 4. Appraisal and coping

Transactional theories of stress emphasise appraisal of demands, resources, meaning, and available responses [@lazarusfolkman1984]. Coping research distinguishes broad families such as problem-focused action, emotion-focused regulation, avoidance, acceptance, and support seeking, while recognising that the adaptiveness of a response depends on context [@carver1989; @folkman2004].

Strict negotiation can be understood as one form of problem-focused coping, but the categories are not identical. A person can solve a problem unilaterally, repair damage, gather information, or leave. Negotiation requires an influenceable counterpart, interdependence, communication, and a contingent attempt to alter the terms.

A useful empirical question is therefore not “Is negotiation coping?” It clearly can be. The useful question is whether the structural features identified by the Demure Fulcrum predict a coherent response beyond existing coping dimensions.

## 5. Control, controllability, and transformability

Perceived control changes emotional and behavioural responses, but its effects are not simple [@thompson1981; @skinner1996]. Learned-helplessness research shows that uncontrollability can alter later motivation and regulation, while later work emphasises neural detection of control and the possibility of recovery [@maier2016].

The Demure Fulcrum introduces *transformability* as a related but narrower appraisal. Direct control means that an actor can produce an outcome. Transformability means that an actor may alter the terms through another actor or process. A patient may lack control over illness while retaining influence over treatment choices. An employee may lack authority over a reorganisation while negotiating transition time, duties, severance, or references.

This distinction creates a testable comparison. Experimental scenarios can hold objective control constant while manipulating whether a counterpart can exchange information or alter terms. If strict negotiation rises with transformability after control is held constant, the construct gains specificity.

## 6. Agency and self-determination

Agency has been conceptualised as intentional action, perceived efficacy, temporal orientation, and the practical capacity to pursue valued outcomes [@bandura2001; @bandura2006; @emirbayer1998]. Capability approaches also emphasise that formal choice is insufficient when social and material conditions make options unusable [@sen1999; @kabeer1999]. Self-determination research highlights autonomy, competence, and relatedness as important psychological needs [@deci2000].

These traditions support a graded view. A person may possess intention but lack resources, or may receive a formal offer with no viable alternative. The Demure Fulcrum therefore operationalises agency through behaviour and structure: interest representation, alternative generation, counterproposal, information access, appeal, delay, exit, and enforceable commitment.

This prevents two errors. One is romantic individualism: assuming every threat contains sufficient leverage if only the person thinks correctly. The other is complete passivity: overlooking small but consequential forms of influence within constrained systems.

## 7. Emotion regulation and reappraisal

Emotion regulation includes strategies that influence which emotions arise, when they arise, and how they are experienced or expressed [@gross1998]. Cognitive reappraisal changes the interpretation of an event and recruits distributed control and meaning-related systems [@ochsner2005; @buhle2014]. Reappraisal ability can moderate relations between stress and later symptoms, especially when circumstances permit useful reinterpretation [@troy2010].

Reappraisal can enable negotiation by reducing perceived absoluteness, revealing the other party's constraints, or separating immediate emotion from long-term interest. It is not sufficient for strict negotiation. A person can reappraise an irreversible loss without any external exchange. Similarly, skilled bargaining can occur while emotion remains intense.

Research should therefore measure regulation separately rather than treating it as the neural essence of the Demure Fulcrum.

## 8. Social responses under stress

The tend-and-befriend model challenged accounts that focused only on solitary confrontation or escape [@taylor2000; @taylor2006]. Affiliative behaviour can build safety, recruit allies, and buffer stress. Experimental work also shows that acute stress can increase some prosocial behaviours under specific conditions [@vondawans2012].

Affiliation and negotiation can support one another. An ally may create leverage, a trusted relationship may permit honest information exchange, and a mediator may stabilise communication. Yet affiliation can occur without bargaining, and bargaining can be hostile. They should remain distinct variables.

## 9. Appeasement, submission, and fawn language

Appeasement may reduce threat by signalling non-competition, compliance, or submission. Evolutionary and clinical accounts connect appeasement to entrapment, coercive control, hostage reactions, and trauma [@cantorprice2007]. The term *fawn* became popular through trauma-oriented clinical literature [@walker2013], but it is not a settled psychobiological category with a unique hormonal or neural profile.

The behaviour should not be moralised. In severe power asymmetry, unilateral compliance may be the safest option. The proposed distinction from negotiation concerns observable conditionality and residual choice, not courage or worth.

Candidate indicators of negotiation include counterproposals, reciprocal concessions, information seeking, explicit interests, and preserved alternatives. Candidate indicators of appeasement include unconditional concession, self-silencing, and behaviour aimed primarily at immediate threat reduction. Mixed cases should be expected.

## 10. Polyvagal Theory: relevance and limits

Polyvagal Theory offers an influential account linking autonomic state, safety, immobilisation, mobilisation, and social engagement [@porges2007; @porges2022]. It has also received serious criticism regarding its evolutionary narrative, anatomical premises, and interpretation of respiratory sinus arrhythmia [@grossmantaylor2007; @taylor2015; @grossman2017; @grossman2023]. The critiques differ in scope, but together they justify treating the framework as contested rather than settled.

The Demure Fulcrum does not require resolution of that dispute. The general proposition that autonomic regulation affects communication is compatible with many traditions. Specific claims about a uniquely mammalian ventral-vagal hierarchy should not be treated as established foundations for negotiation. If those specific claims are revised or rejected, the functional distinction between choosing within a situation and attempting to transform its terms remains independently testable.

## 11. Direct implications for the framework

The literature supports several constraints:

- threat responses are dynamic rather than a fixed three-item list;
- the same outward behaviour can serve different functions;
- stress can impair, redirect, or sometimes sharpen behaviour depending on context;
- appraisal of control and available coping options matters;
- social affiliation is a genuine response family but is not identical to bargaining;
- reappraisal can support negotiation without defining it;
- appeasement can be adaptive and should not be framed as personal failure;
- no unique neural or hormonal signature for the Demure Fulcrum has been established.

## 12. What the literature does not establish

It does not show that negotiation is an autonomic response equivalent to fight, flight, or freeze. It does not show that negotiation is uniquely human, always conscious, necessarily ethical, or universally beneficial. It does not demonstrate that the proposed indicators form one latent variable. Those questions require direct construct validation and behavioural experimentation.
